Course Syllabus — Summer Session 2026

STAT 35000 is offered in the Summer Session as a single asynchronous online section running June 15 – August 7, 2026 (the second eight weeks). There are no scheduled class meetings: you work through the lecture videos, readings, and assignments on your own, paced by weekly deadlines. The session covers the same thirteen chapters as the 16-week course and uses the same webbook and video resources, but compresses them into eight weeks and delivers all three exams online through Respondus LockDown Browser.

Your Official Syllabus

The official syllabus for your section is available through Brightspace and governs in case of any discrepancy.

A Full Semester in Eight Weeks

Expect 15–20 focused hours per week, with some weeks considerably heavier than others. Video time alone ranges from 2 to 5+ hours depending on the week. The compressed calendar leaves little room for catching up, so use the lighter weeks to build a buffer for the heavy ones.

Course Description & Prerequisites

(Credit Hours: 3.00) A data-oriented introduction to the fundamental concepts and methods of applied statistics. Exploratory analysis of data. Sample design and experimental design. Probability distributions and simulation. Sampling distributions. The reasoning of statistical inference. Confidence intervals and tests for one and two samples. Inference for regression and correlation. Essential use is made of statistical software throughout.

Prerequisites: Calculus 2. You must demonstrate proficiency in basic derivative and integral calculus in both homework and exams. More advanced topics may be introduced, but assessment will not exceed a Calculus 2 level of difficulty.

Students who do not engage in academically related activities by the published deadlines may have their financial aid eligibility reviewed by the University, regardless of any later make-up arrangements.

For anticipated conflicts such as religious observance or University-sponsored activities, notify the instructor as far in advance as possible.

How to Succeed in This Course

Each week follows the same four steps:

  1. Absorb (≈6–8 hours). Complete the assigned reading in the webbook and watch the segmented lecture videos. Use the search bar and timeline in the video viewer to target topics you find challenging.

  2. Apply (≈6–8 hours). Work the Edfinity homework on scratch paper first, then submit. Review the computer assignment tutorials before attempting the assignments.

  3. Assess (≈20 minutes). Take the timed weekly quiz in Brightspace.

  4. Review (≈2 hours). Check posted solutions, revisit video segments on hard topics, and post or answer questions on the peer board.

A few habits that make the difference:

  • Check Brightspace weekly for deadlines and announcements. The compressed calendar leaves little room for catching up.

  • Work ahead during the lighter weeks to build a buffer for the heavier ones — the workload is not uniform.

  • Keep tidy notes as you go. The exams are open-note, and notes written the night before are not as useful as notes written while you learned the material.

  • Lean on the peer discussion board. Your classmates are your fastest source of help; instructor replies typically come in the late afternoon on weekdays, and weekend replies may take longer than 24 hours.

Academic Integrity & AI Policy

You are expected to uphold the Purdue Honor Pledge: “As a boilermaker pursuing academic excellence, I pledge to be honest and true in all that I do. Accountable together — we are Purdue.”

Cheating on an exam results in an F in the course — this includes communicating exam details to students who have not yet taken it and using non-approved materials during the exam. Cheating on homework, computer assignments, and quizzes results in a zero for that assignment. All incidents are addressed by the course coordinator and referred to OSRR (765-494-1250, osrr@purdue.edu) for review at the university level; additional penalties, up to removal from the university, may be considered. Copying previously posted keys (including keys found on the internet) and using services like Chegg, Course Hero, or Quizlet to obtain answers are cheating. Suspected breaches can be reported to integrity@purdue.edu or 765-494-8778 (anonymous submissions accepted).

Good collaboration — we encourage working together the right way:

  • Start by working every homework problem on your own, then form a small group with others who have also attempted every problem.

  • Discuss ideas and approaches for the harder problems, but finish the homework yourself so your submission reflects your own understanding.

  • For review problems, work individually first, then use the group to discuss concepts and practice problems.

  • Take turns explaining concepts and practice problems to each other.

  • If you need help, ask a TA or tutor — but still do the actual problem yourself.

Academic misconduct — the following are cheating, and both the student who copies and the student who allows copying are penalized:

  • Screen-sharing during an exam, allowing another person to view your screen or be present within range of your camera or microphone, or being in the same physical space as another exam-taker.

  • Dividing up the problems among a group and sharing the work.

  • Posting answers to a homework question publicly on the Brightspace discussion board.

  • Attending or joining group work without having first attempted every problem yourself, copying a partner’s answers, or letting an unprepared partner copy yours.

  • Starting a problem yourself but copying someone else’s solution after getting stuck, or reading someone else’s answers before completing your own work.

  • Having a tutor, TA, or tutoring service (Chegg, Course Hero, etc.) work your problems, including referring to electronically posted materials.

  • Sharing a computer assignment, printing two copies of the same output, or doing the assignment together on one computer.

  • Not keeping your exam covered.

AI Usage Policy

AI tools (such as Anthropic’s Claude, OpenAI’s ChatGPT, or other large language models) may be used to check your reasoning, get hints, understand concepts, debug R code, explore alternative approaches to problems you have already solved, or generate practice problems — after you have attempted the work yourself. They may not be used to generate complete solutions, to submit AI-generated code or text as your own work, to complete assignments without your own substantial effort, or during exams. The course-specific tools Ask Anna (within Edfinity) and LatticeAI fall under the same permitted and prohibited uses; in particular, neither is permitted during exams.

You are responsible for being able to explain any work you submit; homework is practice for exams where AI is not available. If unauthorized AI use is suspected, you may be asked to explain your reasoning verbally or to reproduce comparable work without AI assistance, and an inability to do so will be treated as evidence of a violation. This course does not rely on automated AI-detection software. Violations of this policy are treated as academic misconduct and reported to OSRR.
