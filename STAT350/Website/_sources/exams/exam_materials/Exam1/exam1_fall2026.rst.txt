Problem 1 — True/False  (12 points, 2 points each)
----------------------------------------------------

.. admonition:: Question 1.1  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   A dataset :math:`X` has an interquartile range (IQR) of :math:`\text{IQR}_X`. Each value
   in the dataset is multiplied by :math:`-2`, and then 10 is added, giving new values
   :math:`y = -2x + 10`.

   **True or False:** The IQR of the new values of the dataset :math:`Y` is
   :math:`-2 \cdot \text{IQR}_X + 10`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: FALSE**

      Adding 10 moves both quartiles up by 10, so it cancels in :math:`Q_3 - Q_1`.
      Multiplying by :math:`-2` reverses the order of the values, so the third quartile of
      :math:`Y` comes from the first quartile of :math:`X`, and the first quartile of
      :math:`Y` comes from the third quartile of :math:`X`:

      .. math::

         Q_{3,Y} = -2\,Q_{1,X} + 10, \qquad Q_{1,Y} = -2\,Q_{3,X} + 10.

      Therefore

      .. math::

         \text{IQR}_Y = Q_{3,Y} - Q_{1,Y} = 2\,(Q_{3,X} - Q_{1,X}) = 2 \cdot \text{IQR}_X.

.. admonition:: Question 1.2  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   Let :math:`A` and :math:`B` be two non-empty events from the same sample space
   :math:`\Omega`, both with positive probabilities (:math:`P(A) > 0` and :math:`P(B) > 0`).

   **True or False:** If :math:`A` and :math:`B` are mutually exclusive (disjoint), then
   they cannot be independent events.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: TRUE**

      Disjoint events have :math:`P(A \cap B) = 0`. Independence requires
      :math:`P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)`, and :math:`P(A)P(B) > 0` because both probabilities
      are positive. So :math:`P(A \cap B) \neq P(A)P(B)`, and the events are not
      independent. Equivalently, if :math:`A` occurs then :math:`B` cannot, so
      :math:`P(B \mid A) = 0 \neq P(B)`.

.. admonition:: Question 1.3  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   Let :math:`X` be a discrete random variable with finite mean :math:`\mu` and standard
   deviation :math:`\sigma > 0`. Define a new random variable :math:`Y = 4 - 3X`.

   **True or False:** The standard deviation of :math:`Y` is equal to :math:`-3\sigma`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: FALSE**

      The constant 4 does not change the spread, and the multiplier enters the variance
      squared:

      .. math::

         \text{Var}(Y) = (-3)^2\,\text{Var}(X) = 9\sigma^2,
         \qquad
         \sigma_Y = \sqrt{9\sigma^2} = 3\sigma.

      A standard deviation is never negative, while :math:`-3\sigma < 0` because
      :math:`\sigma > 0`.

.. admonition:: Question 1.4  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   Let :math:`X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)` have :math:`n \geq 1` independent trials and
   success probability :math:`p \in [0, 1]`.

   **True or False:** The variance :math:`\text{Var}(X)` can never exceed
   :math:`\dfrac{n}{4}`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: TRUE**

      The variance is :math:`\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)`. The product :math:`p(1-p)` is
      largest at :math:`p = \tfrac{1}{2}`:

      .. math::

         p(1-p) = \tfrac{1}{4} - \left(p - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 \leq \tfrac{1}{4}.

      Therefore :math:`\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \leq \dfrac{n}{4}`, with equality only when
      :math:`p = \tfrac{1}{2}`.

.. admonition:: Question 1.5  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   Let :math:`X` be a continuous random variable with probability density function
   :math:`f(x)`.

   **True or False:** The density must satisfy :math:`f(x) \leq 1` for all
   :math:`x \in \mathbb{R}`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: FALSE**

      A density must be nonnegative with total area 1 under its curve; its height can
      exceed 1. For example, :math:`X \sim \text{Uniform}(a = 0, b = 0.5)` has

      .. math::

         f(x) = \begin{cases}
            2 & 0 \leq x \leq 0.5 \\
            0 & \text{otherwise}
         \end{cases}

      and its total area is :math:`2 \times 0.5 = 1`. Values of :math:`f(x)` are not
      probabilities; probabilities are areas under :math:`f`.

.. admonition:: Question 1.6  (2 pts)
   :class: note

   Suppose :math:`X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)`.

   **True or False:** The probability that :math:`X` falls within :math:`1.28` standard
   deviations of the mean, :math:`P(\mu - 1.28\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 1.28\sigma)`, is
   :math:`0.80`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: TRUE**

      Standardize with :math:`Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma` and use the standard normal table:

      .. math::

         P(\mu - 1.28\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 1.28\sigma)
         &= P(-1.28 \leq Z \leq 1.28) \\
         &= \Phi(1.28) - \Phi(-1.28) \\
         &= 0.8997 - 0.1003 = 0.7994 \approx 0.80.

Problem 2 — Multiple Choice  (15 points, 3 points each)
---------------------------------------------------------

.. admonition:: Question 2.1  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   A survey of 150 recent Purdue engineering graduates revealed that their starting annual
   salaries had a sample mean of $86,500 and a sample median of $78,000. Based on these
   summary statistics, which of the following statements is most likely true regarding the
   distribution of starting salaries?

   (A) The distribution is symmetric and mound-shaped because engineering salaries are
       standardized.

   (B) The distribution is right-skewed (positively skewed) because a relatively small
       number of exceptionally high salaries pull the sample mean above the sample median.

   (C) The distribution is left-skewed (negatively skewed) because the majority of
       graduates earn less than the mean.

   (D) The sample standard deviation must be equal to zero.

   (E) The interquartile range (IQR) is guaranteed to be larger than the full range of the
       data.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: (B)**

      - **(A) FALSE.** In a symmetric distribution the sample mean and sample median are close; here the sample mean is $8,500 above the sample median.
      - **(B) TRUE.** The sample mean is sensitive to extreme values and the sample median is not. A few very high salaries pull the sample mean above the sample median, which is the pattern of a right-skewed distribution.
      - **(C) FALSE.** In a left-skewed distribution, low values pull the sample mean below the sample median. Here the sample mean is above the sample median.
      - **(D) FALSE.** A standard deviation of zero means every salary is the same, so the sample mean and sample median would be equal.
      - **(E) FALSE.** The IQR can never exceed the range, because :math:`Q_1 \geq \min` and :math:`Q_3 \leq \max`.

.. admonition:: Question 2.2  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   During peak lunch hours at the Purdue Memorial Union, mobile food orders arrive
   according to a Poisson process at an average rate of :math:`0.4` orders per minute.
   What is the probability that exactly :math:`2` orders arrive in a randomly selected
   :math:`5`-minute interval?

   (A) 0.0544

   (B) 0.1465

   (C) 0.2707

   (D) 0.4000

   (E) 0.6767

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: (C)**

      A 5-minute interval has mean :math:`\lambda = 0.4 \times 5 = 2` orders, so the number
      of orders :math:`X` in the interval satisfies :math:`X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda = 2)`:

      .. math::

         P(X = 2) = \frac{e^{-2}\,2^2}{2!} = 2e^{-2} = 0.2707.

.. admonition:: Question 2.3  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   Let :math:`X` be a discrete random variable representing the number of defective
   components found in an electronic assembly pack, with probability mass function:

   .. math::

      P(X = 0) = 0.60, \quad P(X = 1) = 0.30, \quad P(X = 2) = 0.10

   The repair cost (in dollars) for a pack is modeled by the function
   :math:`C(X) = 50 + 20X^2`. What is the expected repair cost :math:`E[C(X)]`?

   (A) $50.00

   (B) $57.00

   (C) $60.00

   (D) $64.00

   (E) $78.00

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: (D)**

      By linearity of expectation, :math:`E[C(X)] = 50 + 20\,E[X^2]`. By LOTUS,

      .. math::

         E[X^2] = 0^2(0.60) + 1^2(0.30) + 2^2(0.10) = 0 + 0.30 + 0.40 = 0.70.

      Therefore :math:`E[C(X)] = 50 + 20(0.70) = 64`, an expected repair cost of $64.00.

.. admonition:: Question 2.4  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   An inspector examines a small shipment of :math:`N = 25` laptops. Unbeknownst to the
   inspector, the shipment contains exactly :math:`4` defective units. The inspector
   randomly selects :math:`n = 5` laptops without replacement and counts the number of
   defective units :math:`X` among the :math:`5` sampled. Which of the following explains
   why :math:`X` does NOT strictly follow a Binomial distribution?

   (A) The draws are dependent because each selection changes which laptops remain in the
       shipment.

   (B) A draw cannot have binary outcomes because defect status is learned only after
       inspection.

   (C) The number of trials is not fixed because the defect count is unknown before
       inspection.

   (D) The expected defect count cannot be computed before the inspector checks the
       selected laptops.

   (E) The variance must exceed the sample size because the inspector samples without
       replacement.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: (A)**

      - **(A) TRUE.** Each laptop drawn changes what remains. The second laptop is defective with probability :math:`3/24` if the first was defective and :math:`4/24` if it was not, so the draws are dependent and the **Independent** criterion of BInS fails.
      - **(B) FALSE.** Each laptop is either defective or not, so every draw has two outcomes. When the status is learned does not matter.
      - **(C) FALSE.** The number of draws is fixed in advance at :math:`n = 5`.
      - **(D) FALSE.** An expected value is computed from the distribution of :math:`X` before any laptop is inspected, and it is not one of the BInS criteria.
      - **(E) FALSE.** Nothing requires the variance of :math:`X` to exceed the sample size, and the variance is not one of the BInS criteria.

.. admonition:: Question 2.5  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   The delivery time :math:`T` (in minutes) for autonomous delivery robots on the Purdue
   campus follows an Exponential distribution with rate :math:`\lambda = 0.10` per minute.
   The dining service promises a delivery fee refund if an order takes longer than
   :math:`c` minutes. If they wish to issue refunds for only the top :math:`2.5\%` slowest
   deliveries, what should the threshold :math:`c` be?

   (A) 27.73 minutes

   (B) 29.96 minutes

   (C) 36.89 minutes

   (D) 39.12 minutes

   (E) 46.05 minutes

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **Answer: (C)**

      For :math:`T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda = 0.10)`, :math:`P(T > t) = e^{-0.10t}` for
      :math:`t \geq 0`. The threshold :math:`c` satisfies :math:`P(T > c) = 0.025`:

      .. math::

         e^{-0.10c} = 0.025
         \quad\Rightarrow\quad
         c = \frac{-\ln(0.025)}{0.10} = \frac{3.6889}{0.10} = 36.89 \text{ minutes}.

Problem 3  (26 points) — HPC Simulation Runtime
-------------------------------------------------

.. admonition:: Problem 3 Setup
   :class: important

   A High-Performance Computing (HPC) cluster at Purdue processes large-scale scientific
   simulations. Every submitted task runs a mandatory one-hour CPU calibration stage before
   its simulation can finish or reach a checkpoint. Let :math:`X` denote the total active
   CPU runtime (in hours), including that calibration stage, of a randomly submitted
   simulation task before completion or checkpointing. Thus, :math:`X` cannot be less than
   one hour. The probability density function (PDF) of :math:`X` is modeled by the
   following piecewise function:

   .. math::

      f(x) = \begin{cases}
         cx, & 1 \leq x \leq 3 \\
         3c, & 3 < x \leq 5 \\
         0, & \text{otherwise}
      \end{cases}

.. admonition:: Question 3a  (8 pts)
   :class: note

   Determine the value of the constant :math:`c` such that :math:`f(x)` is a valid
   probability density function. Show all integration steps clearly.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      The total area under :math:`f` must be 1:

      .. math::

         1 = \int_1^3 cx\,dx + \int_3^5 3c\,dx
           = c\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^3 + 3c\,\big[x\big]_3^5
           = c\left(\frac{9}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\right) + 3c(5 - 3)
           = 4c + 6c = 10c.

      Thus :math:`c = \dfrac{1}{10} = 0.1000`.

.. admonition:: Question 3b  (6 pts)
   :class: note

   The intervals of the Cumulative Distribution Function :math:`F(x)` are shown below.
   Find the expression for each labeled blank (i)–(iv) to complete :math:`F(x)` for all
   real numbers :math:`x \in \mathbb{R}`.

   .. math::

      F(x) = \begin{cases}
         \boxed{\text{(i)}} & x < 1 \\
         \boxed{\text{(ii)}} & 1 \leq x \leq 3 \\
         \boxed{\text{(iii)}} & 3 < x \leq 5 \\
         \boxed{\text{(iv)}} & x > 5
      \end{cases}

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      No probability lies below 1, and all of it lies at or below 5, so the outer blanks
      are **(i)** :math:`0` and **(iv)** :math:`1`. With :math:`c = \tfrac{1}{10}`, the two
      inner cases are:

      .. math::

         \text{(ii)}\quad F(x) = \int_1^x \frac{t}{10}\,dt = \frac{x^2 - 1}{20},

      .. math::

         \text{(iii)}\quad F(x) = F(3) + \int_3^x \frac{3}{10}\,dt
         = \frac{2}{5} + \frac{3(x - 3)}{10} = \frac{3x - 5}{10}.

      The complete CDF is

      .. math::

         F(x) = \begin{cases}
            0 & x < 1 \\[4pt]
            \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{20} & 1 \leq x \leq 3 \\[8pt]
            \dfrac{3x - 5}{10} & 3 < x \leq 5 \\[8pt]
            1 & x > 5
         \end{cases}

.. admonition:: Question 3c  (4 pts)
   :class: note

   What is the probability that a randomly chosen simulation task takes between :math:`2`
   and :math:`4` hours to finish?

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      Using the two inner cases of :math:`F(x)`, :math:`F(4) = \tfrac{7}{10}` and
      :math:`F(2) = \tfrac{3}{20}`. Hence,

      .. math::

         P(2 \leq X \leq 4) = F(4) - F(2) = \frac{7}{10} - \frac{3}{20} = \frac{11}{20} = 0.5500.

.. admonition:: Question 3d  (4 pts)
   :class: note

   Given that a simulation task has already run for at least :math:`2` hours, what is the
   conditional probability that it takes more than :math:`4` hours to finish?

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      Since :math:`\{X > 4\} \subseteq \{X \geq 2\}`, the intersection of the two events is
      :math:`\{X > 4\}`:

      .. math::

         P(X > 4 \mid X \geq 2) = \frac{P(X > 4)}{P(X \geq 2)}
         = \frac{1 - F(4)}{1 - F(2)} = \frac{0.3000}{0.8500} = \frac{6}{17} \approx 0.3529.

.. admonition:: Question 3e  (4 pts)
   :class: note

   Find the median execution duration :math:`\tilde{\mu}` (in hours) of a simulation task.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      Since :math:`F(3) = 0.4000 < 0.50`, the median lies in :math:`3 < x \leq 5`. Solve

      .. math::

         F(\tilde{\mu}) = \frac{3\tilde{\mu} - 5}{10} = 0.50
         \quad\Rightarrow\quad
         \tilde{\mu} = \frac{10}{3} \approx 3.3333 \text{ hours}.

Problem 4  (26 points) — Autonomous Shuttle Alerts
----------------------------------------------------

.. admonition:: Problem 4 Setup
   :class: important

   Purdue's autonomous electric shuttle fleet navigates three campus routes: Route A
   (Engineering Quad), Route B (Stadium Drive), and Route C (Discovery Park).

   Shuttles are assigned randomly to routes based on campus demand:

   - Route A: assigned :math:`50\%` of the time, :math:`P(A) = 0.50`
   - Route B: assigned :math:`30\%` of the time, :math:`P(B) = 0.30`
   - Route C: assigned :math:`20\%` of the time, :math:`P(C) = 0.20`

   During operation, high pedestrian density or sun glare may trigger a safety sensor
   alert (:math:`S`). Telemetry data shows:

   - On Route A: :math:`P(S \mid A) = 0.08`
   - On Route B: :math:`P(S \mid B) = 0.15`
   - On Route C: :math:`P(S \mid C) = 0.25`

.. admonition:: Question 4a  (4 pts)
   :class: note

   Calculate the joint probabilities for the three alert scenarios:
   :math:`P(A \cap S)`, :math:`P(B \cap S)`, and :math:`P(C \cap S)`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      Apply :math:`P(R \cap S) = P(R)\,P(S \mid R)` for each route :math:`R`:

      .. math::

         P(A \cap S) &= (0.50)(0.08) = 0.0400 \\
         P(B \cap S) &= (0.30)(0.15) = 0.0450 \\
         P(C \cap S) &= (0.20)(0.25) = 0.0500

.. admonition:: Question 4b  (6 pts)
   :class: note

   Calculate the overall probability :math:`P(S)` that a randomly selected shuttle trip
   experiences a sensor alert.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      The three routes partition the sample space, so by the Law of Partitions,

      .. math::

         P(S) = P(A \cap S) + P(B \cap S) + P(C \cap S) = 0.0400 + 0.0450 + 0.0500 = 0.1350.

.. admonition:: Question 4c  (8 pts)
   :class: note

   Suppose a shuttle trip is known to have experienced a sensor alert (:math:`S`). Compute
   the probability that this shuttle was traveling on Route A.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      By Bayes' Theorem,

      .. math::

         P(A \mid S) = \frac{P(A \cap S)}{P(S)} = \frac{0.0400}{0.1350} = \frac{8}{27} \approx 0.2963.

.. admonition:: Question 4d  (5 pts)
   :class: note

   Are the event :math:`\{\text{Shuttle is on Route A}\}` and the event
   :math:`\{\text{Sensor alert occurs}\}` independent? State YES or NO and provide clear
   mathematical justification.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      **NO.** Since :math:`P(S \mid A) = 0.0800 \neq P(S) = 0.1350`, the two events are
      dependent. Equivalently, :math:`P(A \cap S) = 0.0400`, while
      :math:`P(A)\,P(S) = (0.50)(0.1350) = 0.0675`.

.. admonition:: Question 4e  (3 pts)
   :class: note

   Suppose :math:`4` independent shuttle trips on Route C are monitored. What is the
   probability that at least one of these :math:`4` trips experiences a sensor alert?

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      Let :math:`Y` be the number of the 4 trips with an alert. Each trip has an alert or
      not, the trips are independent, :math:`n = 4` is fixed, and each trip has the same
      alert probability :math:`P(S \mid C) = 0.25`, so
      :math:`Y \sim \text{Binomial}(n = 4, p = 0.25)`. Use the complement:

      .. math::

         P(Y \geq 1) = 1 - P(Y = 0) = 1 - (0.75)^4 = 1 - 0.3164 = 0.6836.

Problem 5  (26 points) — Silicon Wafer Thickness
--------------------------------------------------

.. admonition:: Problem 5 Setup
   :class: important

   High-precision silicon wafer substrates manufactured at the Birck Nanotechnology Center
   have a thickness :math:`X` (in micrometers) that follows a Normal distribution with
   unknown mean :math:`\mu` and unknown standard deviation :math:`\sigma`.

   Quality assurance testing on a large production run establishes the following two
   specifications:

   1. Exactly :math:`10\%` of the manufactured wafers have a thickness strictly less than
      :math:`47.812` micrometers: :math:`P(X < 47.812) = 0.10`.
   2. Exactly :math:`5\%` of the manufactured wafers have a thickness strictly greater
      than :math:`53.944` micrometers: :math:`P(X > 53.944) = 0.05`.

   Use a standard normal table with :math:`z` values rounded to two decimal places. If two
   table entries are equally close to a target cumulative area, use the smaller :math:`z`
   value.

.. admonition:: Question 5a  (8 pts)
   :class: note

   Calculate the population mean :math:`\mu` and standard deviation :math:`\sigma`.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      From the standard normal table, :math:`\Phi(-1.28) = 0.1003` is the entry closest to
      0.10, so :math:`z = -1.28`. Specification 2 gives a cumulative area of
      :math:`P(X \leq 53.944) = 0.95`; :math:`\Phi(1.64) = 0.9495` and
      :math:`\Phi(1.65) = 0.9505` are equally close, and the stated tie rule selects
      :math:`z = 1.64`. Thus,

      .. math::

         47.812 = \mu - 1.28\sigma, \qquad 53.944 = \mu + 1.64\sigma.

      Subtracting the first equation from the second gives :math:`6.132 = 2.92\sigma`, so

      .. math::

         \sigma = \frac{53.944 - 47.812}{1.64 + 1.28} = \frac{6.132}{2.92} = 2.1 \text{ micrometers},

      .. math::

         \mu = 47.812 + 1.28(2.1) = 50.5 \text{ micrometers}.

.. admonition:: Fallback values for 5(b)–5(d)
   :class: important

   If you cannot determine :math:`\mu` and :math:`\sigma` in 5(a), you may use
   :math:`\mu = 50` micrometers and :math:`\sigma = 2.0` micrometers for parts 5(b)–5(d).
   **These are not the correct answers to 5(a)**; they are provided only so you can
   continue with the rest of the problem. If you use them, say so clearly in your work.
   You will not receive credit for 5(a) by using these values.

.. admonition:: Question 5b  (6 pts)
   :class: note

   Find the probability that a randomly chosen wafer has a thickness between :math:`47`
   micrometers and :math:`53` micrometers.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      With :math:`\mu = 50.5` and :math:`\sigma = 2.1`, the endpoints standardize to

      .. math::

         z = \frac{47 - 50.5}{2.1} = -1.6667 \approx -1.67,
         \qquad
         z = \frac{53 - 50.5}{2.1} = 1.1905 \approx 1.19.

      The table gives :math:`\Phi(-1.67) = 0.0475` and :math:`\Phi(1.19) = 0.8830`, so

      .. math::

         P(47 \leq X \leq 53) = \Phi(1.19) - \Phi(-1.67) = 0.8830 - 0.0475 = 0.8355.

      **With the fallback values,** the endpoints standardize to :math:`z = -1.50` and
      :math:`z = 1.50`, and :math:`P(47 \leq X \leq 53) = 0.9332 - 0.0668 = 0.8664`.

.. admonition:: Question 5c  (6 pts)
   :class: note

   Determine the first quartile :math:`Q_1`, the third quartile :math:`Q_3`, and the
   population Interquartile Range (IQR) for wafer thickness.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      The standard normal table gives :math:`\Phi(-0.67) = 0.2514` and
      :math:`\Phi(0.67) = 0.7486`, the entries closest to 0.25 and 0.75, so use quartile
      :math:`z` values :math:`\pm 0.67`:

      .. math::

         Q_1 &\approx \mu - 0.67\sigma = 50.5 - 0.67(2.1) = 49.093 \text{ micrometers} \\
         Q_3 &\approx \mu + 0.67\sigma = 50.5 + 0.67(2.1) = 51.907 \text{ micrometers} \\
         \text{IQR} &= Q_3 - Q_1 = 2.814 \text{ micrometers}

      **With the fallback values,** :math:`Q_1 \approx 48.66` micrometers,
      :math:`Q_3 \approx 51.34` micrometers, and :math:`\text{IQR} \approx 2.68`
      micrometers.

.. admonition:: Question 5d  (6 pts)
   :class: note

   Calculate the population lower and upper inner fences. Using these fences as a
   quality-control rule, would a manufactured wafer with a thickness of :math:`44.20`
   micrometers be flagged as a potential outlier requiring further inspection? Explain in
   a complete sentence.

   .. dropdown:: Solution
      :class-container: sd-border-success

      .. math::

         \text{Lower inner fence} &= Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR} = 49.093 - 1.5(2.814) = 44.872 \text{ micrometers} \\
         \text{Upper inner fence} &= Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} = 51.907 + 1.5(2.814) = 56.128 \text{ micrometers}

      Because :math:`44.20` micrometers is below the lower inner fence of :math:`44.872`
      micrometers, the wafer would be flagged as a potential outlier.

      **With the fallback values,** the fences are :math:`44.64` and :math:`55.36`
      micrometers. Because :math:`44.20 < 44.64`, the wafer would again be flagged as a
      potential outlier.
